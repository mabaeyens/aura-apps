Aura · hero study

The hero should be the sky, not a panel on it

You built a living sky. The hero card covers its best part with a data table. Here's how to let the life through.

The Phase-F sun disc gave the background genuine life — light that sits where the sun really is, warming from dawn to a gold noon to an orange dusk. But the hero is still a frosted rectangle of numbers pinned over the top of that scene: it reads as a dashboard widget, not as today's weather. The fix isn't more data or a bigger icon — it's changing the hero's relationship to the sky. Three directions, then a recommendation, all built to keep the static-render / no-animation battery rule from Phase G.

It's competent. That's the problem.

Every number is legible and nothing truncates — but the card is doing the job of a spreadsheet cell, and it sits on the sky rather than belonging to it. Four things drain the life:

Occlusion

It hides the best pixels

The sun and sky peak at the top of the screen — exactly where the opaque card lands. The living element is covered by the static one.

Told, not shown

Weather as a word

“Despejado” + a stock SF Symbol. A generic multicolor glyph fights the custom flat-vector language of your sky.

Flat hierarchy

One voice, one weight

Location, condition, wind, humidity all sit at a similar size and weight. Only the temperature is loud; nothing else breathes.

Container

A box, not a scene

The frosted rectangle frames the data like a widget. The reference apps you shared feel immersive because the top of the screen is the weather.

A · Dissolve the card

Let the sky be the hero

Remove the top card entirely. The location becomes a quiet eyebrow, the temperature floats huge and directly on the scene, and the sun disc is the actual focal element beside it. A soft top-and-bottom scrim (gradient, not a box) buys legibility without walls. Máx/Mín and the metrics condense to one unobtrusive line at the base of the sky.

This is the biggest single gain in “life,” and it's the closest to the CARROT / AEMET references — the weather stops being reported and starts being present.

Wins

Maximum atmosphere; the sun does real work; time-of-day drama is felt, not framed.

Costs

Legibility over a bright noon sky needs a careful adaptive scrim; the data still has to live somewhere below.

Keep a card, make it a scene

Retain the safety of a card, but throw out the tiny SF Symbol. Fill the card's right side with a large custom illustration in the same flat-vector language as the sky — a sun with soft rays, drifting clouds, rain streaks, a moon and stars — driven by the current condition. The card becomes a little window onto today, not a data grid.

Lowest risk: legibility is never in question because the card owns its own background. It's the smallest leap from what ships today.

Wins

Guaranteed contrast; a distinctive illustrated identity; reuses your existing scene vocabulary.

Costs

Still a box on the sky — the occlusion problem remains; you now maintain two weather scenes (card + sky).

Confidence through type & a sentence

Dramatize the hierarchy: an enormous temperature, the place and time as a hairline eyebrow, and — the real move — a human one-line summary derived from the forecast (“Caluroso y despejado; se nubla al caer la tarde”). The numbers retreat into a single condensed glass strip at the base. Life comes from a confident voice, not more widgets.

My recommendation is A married to C: dissolve the card (A) so the sky and sun carry the top, and float an editorial temperature + human sentence (C) over an adaptive scrim, with one thin glass strip holding Máx/Mín/wind/humidity. It keeps every current data point, removes the occluding box, and turns the sun disc into the hero it was built to be.

Wins

Immersive and legible; a memorable voice; the whole scene reads at a glance before any number.

Costs

The summary sentence needs a small rules engine mapping AEMET fields → phrasing; the scrim must adapt to sky brightness.
